An automation can run perfectly for three months and still cause a serious problem on the fourth.

A customer submits an order, but the notification never arrives. A form submission creates two records instead of one. An invoice gets sent twice. A workflow stops running because an API connection has expired, and nobody notices for a week.

The automation itself wasn't necessarily badly built. The problem is that it was built to work when everything goes right.

That's not enough.

When a business depends on an automated process, it needs to handle the days when something goes wrong. A useful workflow doesn't just complete its task. It also needs a sensible way to deal with failure.

Here's what that actually involves.

## The mistake: treating a successful test as proof

Most automation projects follow a familiar pattern.

You connect two applications, configure the steps, send a test submission, and see the expected result. The workflow works. You turn it on and move on to the next task.

But a successful test only proves that one particular run worked under one particular set of conditions.

It doesn't prove that:

- The workflow can handle missing information.
- An application outage won't interrupt the process.
- An expired connection will be noticed.
- A failed step won't create duplicate records when retried.
- Someone will know what to do when the automation stops.

Consider a simple contact form automation.

A visitor submits a form. The workflow sends the details to a spreadsheet, creates a contact record, and emails the business owner.

Now imagine the spreadsheet service is temporarily unavailable.

The workflow reaches the spreadsheet step and fails. Depending on how it's configured, the email might never be sent either. The visitor sees a successful form submission, but the business receives nothing.

From the visitor's perspective, the form worked. From the business's perspective, a potential customer disappeared.

That's the kind of problem a test submission won't reveal.

## Five ways business automations commonly fail

Not every failure is complicated. Most come from a handful of predictable situations.

### 1. An application becomes unavailable

Your automation depends on services you don't control.

An email provider might have an outage. A payment service might respond slowly. A CRM might temporarily reject requests. Even a short interruption can stop a workflow.

A reliable automation should have a defined response when a service doesn't respond.

For temporary problems, that might mean trying again after a short delay. For longer outages, it might mean recording the failed operation and alerting someone.

What it shouldn't do is silently abandon the task.

### 2. An API connection expires

Many automations connect to other services through authenticated accounts.

Those connections can expire. Passwords change, access gets revoked, permissions are updated, and providers sometimes require accounts to reconnect.

The workflow may have worked every day until the connection stopped being valid.

This is particularly dangerous when the automation doesn't produce an obvious error notification.

A useful setup should make connection failures visible. If an important workflow hasn't completed successfully, someone should know.

### 3. The incoming data changes

Automations are often built around assumptions about the information they receive.

For example, a workflow might expect every form submission to contain:

- A name
- An email address
- A service selection
- A message

Then someone changes the form and makes the message field optional. Or a third-party service changes the format of a date. Or a customer enters information in an unexpected way.

The workflow may fail because a required value is missing, or worse, continue with incorrect information.

This is why input validation matters. Before processing important data, check that the fields the next steps depend on actually exist and contain usable values.

### 4. A retry creates duplicate work

Retries are useful, but they can introduce another problem.

Imagine an order automation that sends a request to a payment-related service. The service processes the request, but the response doesn't reach the workflow.

The automation sees a failure and tries again.

If the second request is treated as a completely new operation, the same action could happen twice.

This is why important workflows need duplicate protection. A unique order ID, submission ID, or transaction reference can help identify whether an operation has already been processed.

Retrying should not mean blindly repeating every action.

### 5. Nobody notices the workflow has stopped

This is arguably the most expensive failure.

A workflow doesn't always fail with a dramatic error. Sometimes it simply stops receiving data.

For example, a business normally receives 15 enquiries a week through its website. Suddenly, it receives none.

The owner might assume business is quiet. The actual problem could be a broken form integration.

Monitoring should look at more than error messages. For important processes, unusual inactivity can be a warning too.

If a workflow normally runs every day and hasn't run for several days, that deserves attention.

## What a reliable automation should include

You don't need an enterprise monitoring system for every small workflow. But important automations should have a few basic safeguards.

### 1. A clear failure path

Decide what should happen when a step fails.

For example:

- Retry temporary errors where repeating the action is safe.
- Stop processing when required information is missing.
- Save failed submissions somewhere they can be reviewed.
- Notify the responsible person when the workflow cannot continue.

The important part is that failure has a defined outcome.

A workflow should never leave you wondering whether a task was completed.

### 2. Duplicate protection

Before creating records, sending important messages, or processing transactions, consider whether the same request could arrive more than once.

Use a unique identifier wherever possible.

For example, if a customer submits the same order twice because they clicked the button again, the automation should be able to recognize that both submissions refer to the same order.

This is often called idempotency: repeating an operation should not accidentally produce additional results.

You don't need to use the technical term in every project, but you do need to think about the problem.

### 3. Error notifications that explain the problem

A notification that simply says "Workflow failed" isn't particularly helpful.

A useful alert should identify:

- Which workflow failed.
- Which operation failed.
- When it happened.
- Whether the task was retried.
- Where the failed record can be reviewed.

Don't send every technical detail to a business owner. Give them enough information to understand what happened and who needs to investigate.

For a technical person maintaining the workflow, keep the detailed execution information available separately.

### 4. A place for failed records

Not every failed operation should be discarded.

If a customer enquiry couldn't be added to the CRM, save enough information to process it later.

This could be a separate spreadsheet, database table, or error queue, depending on the workflow.

The goal is simple: a temporary technical problem shouldn't permanently erase a customer's request.

Be careful with sensitive information, though. Failed records still need appropriate access controls and retention rules.

### 5. A manual fallback

What happens if the automation is unavailable for an entire day?

For a low-priority task, the answer might be to wait. For customer enquiries, orders, or appointment requests, waiting may not be acceptable.

There should be a way to continue the essential business process manually.

That doesn't mean abandoning automation. It means making sure the business can still operate without it.

## An example: making a contact form automation more reliable

Let's return to the contact form example.

A basic workflow might look like this:

1. A visitor submits the form.
2. The submission is added to a spreadsheet.
3. A notification email is sent to the owner.
4. A confirmation is recorded.

That is a reasonable starting point. But I'd make a few changes before relying on it for real enquiries.

**First, validate the submission.**

Check that the required fields exist and that the email address is usable. Don't let incomplete data move through the entire workflow.

**Second, assign a unique submission ID.**

This makes it easier to recognize repeated submissions and avoid creating duplicate records.

**Third, handle each important step deliberately.**

If the spreadsheet is unavailable, save the submission for later processing. Don't simply discard it because one service is temporarily down.

**Fourth, notify the owner when something needs attention.**

A failed submission should be visible. The owner shouldn't have to discover the problem by noticing that enquiries have disappeared.

**Finally, keep a record of what happened.**

Record whether the submission was received, processed, or failed. That makes troubleshooting much easier than trying to reconstruct events from memory.

The result isn't just a workflow that sends form data to a spreadsheet. It's a process that has a reasonable chance of recovering when one of its dependencies fails.

## How much reliability does a small business actually need?

Not every automation needs the same level of protection.

A workflow that adds a label to a personal task list doesn't need the same safeguards as one that processes customer orders.

I'd divide them into three groups.

**Low-impact automations**

Examples: organizing personal notes, formatting internal documents, or moving non-essential files.

A basic error notification may be enough. If something fails, the consequences are minor.

**Business-support automations**

Examples: updating a CRM, sending internal notifications, or preparing weekly reports.

These should have error handling, execution history, and a way to recover missing records.

**Business-critical automations**

Examples: handling orders, customer enquiries, payment-related processes, or appointment bookings.

These need stronger validation, duplicate protection, clear failure alerts, record keeping, and a manual fallback.

The more money, customer trust, or important data involved, the more carefully the workflow should be designed.

## Before putting an automation into production

Use this checklist before depending on a workflow:

- [ ] What happens if one connected service is unavailable?
- [ ] What happens if required information is missing?
- [ ] Can a retry accidentally repeat an important action?
- [ ] Where do failed records go?
- [ ] Who receives an error notification?
- [ ] Can someone investigate what happened later?
- [ ] Is there a manual way to complete the essential task?
- [ ] Has the workflow been tested with invalid data and failed connections?

You don't have to solve every possible failure before launching. But you should know which failures matter and what the workflow will do about them.

## The uncomfortable truth

An automation that works 99 times and silently loses the 100th customer enquiry is not necessarily a good automation.

The number of successful runs is only part of the story. What happens during the unsuccessful runs matters just as much.

This is one reason I prefer building workflows around the actual business process instead of simply connecting applications and calling the job finished.

A few extra checks at the beginning can prevent hours of investigation later. More importantly, they can prevent customers from being affected by problems they never knew existed.

Build the workflow for the normal day. Then build the safeguards for the day something goes wrong.

That's when you find out whether the automation is actually reliable.
