Your new website is live. The pages look good, the domain works, and you've finally got something to share with customers.

So what's next?

For many business owners, the answer is surprisingly simple: nothing.

They publish the website, share the link once or twice, and wait for visitors, enquiries, and sales to arrive.

But a website launch isn't the finish line. It's the point where your website starts doing its actual job.

You need to make sure everything works, help search engines discover your pages, understand how visitors use the site, and fix problems before they become expensive.

You don't need to spend the next month redesigning everything or checking analytics every hour.

You need a clear plan.

Here's what I recommend doing during the first 30 days after launching a business website.

## Day 1: Test the website like a customer

Before worrying about SEO or traffic, make sure the website works.

You already know how the website is supposed to behave because you were involved in building it. But your visitors don't know that.

They'll click whatever looks useful, fill out forms, open pages, and expect everything to work.

Start by testing the most important parts of the website.

### Check your contact form

Submit a real test enquiry.

Make sure:

- The form accepts valid information.
- Required fields work correctly.
- Invalid information produces a useful error.
- The submission reaches the correct email address.
- The visitor sees a clear confirmation message.

Don't just check whether the button works. Confirm that the actual enquiry arrives.

If your business depends on enquiries, a broken contact form can quietly cost you potential customers.

### Test every important link

Open your navigation menu and click through the main pages.

Check your:

- Homepage buttons.
- Service pages.
- Contact page.
- Email links.
- Phone links.
- Social media links.
- Footer navigation.

A button that looks clickable but leads nowhere is still a broken part of your website.

### Check mobile properly

Open the website on your phone, not just the mobile preview inside your browser.

Look for:

- Text that's difficult to read.
- Buttons that are too small or too close together.
- Images that overflow the screen.
- Menus that don't open correctly.
- Sections that require unnecessary horizontal scrolling.

Most importantly, try completing the main action on mobile.

If visitors are supposed to contact you, try submitting the contact form from your phone.

## Days 2–3: Make sure Google can discover your website

Your website being live doesn't automatically mean every page is appearing in Google Search.

Google needs to discover, crawl, and potentially index your pages. These are different steps, and indexing isn't guaranteed just because a page exists.

Start with Google Search Console.

[Google Search Console](https://search.google.com/search-console/about) is a free tool that helps website owners understand how their pages appear in Google Search and identify certain indexing problems.

### Set up Search Console

If you haven't already:

1. Add your website as a property.
2. Verify that you own the website.
3. Open the Sitemaps section.
4. Submit your sitemap, if your website has one.
5. Check the Page Indexing report when data becomes available.

Your sitemap is usually available at a URL such as:

`https://example.com/sitemap.xml`

The exact location depends on your platform.

If you're using WordPress, your SEO plugin or WordPress setup may already generate one. If you're using Next.js, you may need to configure the sitemap yourself.

Submitting a sitemap helps Google discover your URLs, but it doesn't guarantee that every page will be indexed.

### Inspect your important pages

Use the URL Inspection tool in Search Console to check your homepage and important service pages.

If a page isn't indexed, look for possible reasons:

- The page has a `noindex` directive.
- The page is blocked from crawling.
- Google hasn't discovered it yet.
- The page returns an error.
- Google considers it a duplicate or alternate URL.

You can test the live URL and request indexing when appropriate.

Don't repeatedly request indexing every few hours. Google needs time to process pages, and repeated requests don't guarantee faster results.

## Days 4–7: Check the technical basics

Now that you've tested the website and started the discovery process, spend some time checking the technical details.

You don't need to become a technical SEO expert. Just make sure the important foundations are in place.

### Confirm HTTPS works

Your website should load securely over HTTPS.

Try opening the HTTP version of your domain and confirm that it redirects to the correct HTTPS version.

Also check that your pages don't show browser security warnings.

### Check your page titles and descriptions

Open your main pages and make sure they have meaningful titles and descriptions.

For example, a service page titled:

"Home"

doesn't tell search engines or visitors much.

A clearer title might be:

"WordPress Website Development for Small Businesses"

Write titles that accurately describe the page. Don't stuff them with unrelated keywords.

### Check your website speed

Use [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/) to test your important pages.

Pay attention to:

- Large images.
- Unnecessary scripts.
- Slow-loading sections.
- Layout shifts.
- Poor mobile performance.

Don't obsess over getting a perfect score.

A score is useful for identifying problems, but the actual experience matters more. A website that loads quickly, displays correctly, and lets visitors complete their tasks is more useful than one that achieves a perfect score while still being difficult to use.

### Make sure backups exist

If your website uses WordPress or another system that requires maintenance, confirm that backups are configured.

Know where the backups are stored and how you would restore the website if something went wrong.

A backup that exists but cannot be restored isn't much help.

## Week 2: Start measuring what visitors actually do

By the second week, you should have the technical basics covered.

Now it's time to understand whether people are using the website.

You don't need ten analytics tools. Start with one suitable analytics setup and track a few meaningful actions.

For many small business websites, these are enough:

| Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Visitors | Whether people are reaching the website |
| Important page views | Which pages attract attention |
| Contact form submissions | Whether visitors are enquiring |
| Button clicks | Whether visitors use important calls to action |
| Traffic sources | Where visitors are coming from |

If you use Google Analytics, configure it properly and check that your own test visits aren't being mistaken for customer activity where practical.

The most important thing is to track actions that matter to your business.

For example, a service business should care more about qualified enquiries than about how many people viewed its homepage.

An online store should care about product views, add-to-cart actions, and completed purchases.

A portfolio website may care about project views and contact clicks.

Choose metrics based on the purpose of the website.

### Don't panic if the numbers are small

A new website may receive very little traffic during its first few weeks.

That doesn't automatically mean something is wrong.

You may not have promoted it yet. Google may still be discovering your pages. Your business may simply have a small audience.

At this stage, focus on making sure tracking works and collecting useful information.

A small amount of reliable data is better than a large amount of meaningless data.

## Week 3: Improve the pages that matter most

By the third week, you should have a better understanding of your website's structure and possibly some early visitor data.

This is a good time to review your most important pages.

Start with the homepage and your main service or product pages.

Ask yourself:

- Can a visitor understand what the business offers within a few seconds?
- Is it clear who the service is for?
- Does the page explain the value of the service?
- Is there a clear next step?
- Is the contact information easy to find?

You don't need to redesign the entire website.

Sometimes a small improvement makes more sense.

For example, if your homepage says:

"Welcome to our website"

you could replace it with a clearer statement explaining what your business does and who it helps.

If your service page has a long description but no obvious contact button, add a useful call to action.

If visitors can't understand your pricing or process, make that information easier to find.

The goal is to remove confusion, not to change things just because you can.

### Ask someone unfamiliar with the website to use it

This is one of the most useful things you can do.

Ask a friend, colleague, or potential customer to visit the website and complete a simple task.

For example:

"Find the service we offer for small businesses and tell me how you would contact us."

Don't guide them through the website.

Watch where they click and listen to what confuses them.

You may discover problems that you never noticed because you already know how the website works.

## Week 4: Review the results and make a plan

During the final week, review what you've learned.

Open Search Console and look at the available data.

Check:

- Which pages Google has indexed.
- Whether important pages have indexing issues.
- Which search queries are generating impressions.
- Which pages are receiving clicks.
- Whether any technical errors need attention.

Search Console's [Performance report](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7576553) can help you understand how your pages are performing in Google Search.

If your website is new, the data may still be limited. That's normal.

Don't make major decisions based on a handful of visits or a few days of data.

Instead, identify the problems you can actually confirm.

For example:

**Problem:** Your service page is receiving impressions but very few clicks.

Possible action: Review whether the page title and description clearly communicate the service.

**Problem:** Visitors reach your contact page but don't submit the form.

Possible action: Test the form, simplify unnecessary fields, and make the next step clearer.

**Problem:** Google hasn't indexed an important page.

Possible action: Check whether the page is accessible, linked from your website, and allowed to be indexed.

**Problem:** Visitors are leaving because the page loads slowly.

Possible action: Review large images, unnecessary scripts, and other performance issues.

Make a short list of improvements and prioritize the ones that affect visitors or business results.

## Your first 30-day website checklist

Here's a simple checklist you can use after launching any business website.

### Launch day

- [ ] Test the contact form.
- [ ] Check important buttons and links.
- [ ] Test the website on mobile.
- [ ] Confirm HTTPS works.
- [ ] Check the main pages for obvious errors.

### First week

- [ ] Set up Google Search Console.
- [ ] Submit your sitemap.
- [ ] Inspect important URLs.
- [ ] Check page titles and descriptions.
- [ ] Test website speed.
- [ ] Confirm backups are working.

### Second week

- [ ] Set up analytics if appropriate.
- [ ] Test important conversion events.
- [ ] Check whether tracking is recording correctly.
- [ ] Review the website's main visitor actions.

### Third week

- [ ] Review the homepage and service pages.
- [ ] Ask someone unfamiliar with the website to test it.
- [ ] Fix confusing content or navigation.
- [ ] Improve the most important calls to action.

### Fourth week

- [ ] Review Search Console data.
- [ ] Check indexing and technical issues.
- [ ] Review available visitor data.
- [ ] Identify the three most useful improvements.
- [ ] Create a simple plan for the next month.

## What you should NOT do during the first month

There are a few things I'd avoid.

**Don't redesign the website immediately.**

You just launched it. Give yourself time to identify real problems before making major changes.

**Don't buy every SEO tool you see.**

Start with the free tools that cover your actual needs. You can invest in paid tools later if you have a clear reason.

**Don't expect instant Google rankings.**

Search engines need time to discover and evaluate pages. A new website may take time to gain visibility.

**Don't judge the website only by traffic.**

A website with 100 relevant visitors and five genuine enquiries may be more useful than one with 1,000 visitors and no meaningful results.

**Don't ignore maintenance.**

Keep software updated where necessary, monitor important functionality, and make sure backups are available.

## The website launch is only the beginning

A successful website isn't just one that looks good on launch day.

It's one that continues to work, helps visitors find what they need, and supports the goals of the business.

The first 30 days don't need to be complicated. Test the website, make sure search engines can discover it, measure a few meaningful actions, and improve the parts that are genuinely causing problems.

You don't need to change everything every week.

You need to understand what your website is doing and make thoughtful improvements over time.

If you hired a developer to build your website, ask them what was included after launch. A good handover should explain how to manage the website, where to find important settings, and who to contact if something breaks.

And if you're managing the website yourself, keep this checklist somewhere you can revisit.

Your website is live. Now make sure it's doing its job.
