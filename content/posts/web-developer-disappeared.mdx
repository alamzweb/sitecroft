It's a story that gets told constantly in developer forums and business owner groups.

You hired someone to build your website. It worked. They were responsive during the project. You paid them. They handed it over. And then — six months or a year later — you need a change made, and they don't respond. No email. No phone. No message. Just silence.

Or worse: you never got full admin access in the first place, and now you're locked out of your own website.

This isn't rare. It happens because the freelance world has almost no enforcement. There's no professional board to complain to, no union, no standard for handover. A developer can disappear with your admin credentials and face zero professional consequences.

If you're in this situation right now, here's what to do — step by step.

## First — Stay calm and assess

Before you panic or send angry emails, figure out what you actually have access to and what you don't.

**Check these things:**

1. **Can you log into your hosting account?** (Hostinger, SiteGround, Vercel, wherever the site lives)
2. **Can you log into your domain registrar?** (Where you bought your domain — Namecheap, GoDaddy, etc.)
3. **Can you log into your WordPress admin?** (If it's a WordPress site)
4. **Do you have access to the FTP or SSH credentials?**
5. **Do you have any backup of the site?**

If the answer to all of these is "yes" — good news. Your developer might be slow to respond, but you still have full control. The recovery is annoying but not catastrophic.

If the answer to any of these is "no" — you have a bigger problem. Keep reading.

## If you don't have full access — this is what to do

The three things you need to control on any website are:

- **The domain**
- **The hosting**
- **The code/content**

If your developer controls any of these, you need to get them back.

### Step 1 — Secure the domain first

The domain is the most important. If your developer controls the registrar account, they could let it expire, transfer it, or hold it hostage.

**What to do:**

- **Check who owns the domain** using a WHOIS lookup (whois.icann.org)
- If it's registered to your name — great. You can often recover access by providing ID to the registrar.
- If it's registered to the developer's name — you have a problem. You'll need to contact the registrar and explain the situation. Some registrars will help you recover ownership if you can prove the site is yours (invoices, contracts, correspondence).

**Important:** the domain cannot be transferred or deleted without the current owner's permission. But it also cannot be recovered without convincing the registrar. Act fast if the renewal date is approaching.

### Step 2 — Contact the developer through every channel

Before assuming the worst, actually try to reach them:

- **Every email you have** (personal, business, any address)
- **Every phone number** they've given you
- **Their website contact form**
- **LinkedIn, X, Instagram, any social media**
- **Their current employer** (if you can find it)

Sometimes the answer is mundane — they changed their email, moved countries, or had a personal emergency. Give them 14 days to respond to a polite, direct message before escalating.

**What to write:**

> "Hi [Name], I've been trying to reach you about [specific issue with the site] and haven't heard back. I need admin access to [hosting/domain/WordPress] to make some updates. Can you please respond by [specific date]? If I don't hear back, I'll need to move forward with recovery on my own, which may require contacting the hosting provider."

This serves two purposes: it shows you're serious, and it creates a record that you tried to resolve things amicably.

### Step 3 — Contact the hosting provider

If your site is on hosting the developer set up (in their name), contact the host directly.

**What to explain:**

- You own the website (provide any evidence — invoices, contract, email history)
- You've lost contact with the developer
- You need access to your site

Every major host has a policy for this. Some are helpful, some require strict documentation. The key is proof — the more evidence you have that the site is yours, the better your chances.

**What counts as proof:**

- Contract or agreement with the developer
- Invoices showing you paid for the site
- Email correspondence about the project
- Any documentation that references you as the site owner

If you have none of these, you're going to have a much harder time.

### Step 4 — Recover WordPress admin (if applicable)

If it's a WordPress site and you have hosting access but not WordPress admin:

**Via hosting panel (phpMyAdmin):**

1. Log into your hosting's phpMyAdmin
2. Find the WordPress database
3. Open the `wp_users` table
4. Find your username
5. Change the password field to a new MD5 hash (or reset through the interface if your host supports it)

Your hosting provider's support team can walk you through this if you're not comfortable doing it yourself.

### Step 5 — If you have no access at all

This is the worst-case scenario. If you have no hosting access, no domain access, no WordPress access, and the developer is unreachable — you have three options:

**Option A — Recover via the hosting provider and registrar, as above.** Slow but possible.

**Option B — Rebuild from scratch.** Painful, but if the domain is recoverable, you keep your brand recognition and SEO history. New hosting, new site, redirect everything.

**Option C — Legal action.** If the site has significant value and the developer has clearly acted in bad faith, a lawyer's letter can sometimes produce results. This is expensive and slow, but sometimes the only option.

**Option D — Report to authorities.** If you're outside the developer's jurisdiction (which is common in international freelance work), this rarely produces results. But some countries have consumer protection bodies that will follow up.

## How to prevent this — for next time

Every situation like this is preventable with three simple steps:

### 1. Buy the domain yourself

Never let a developer buy your domain in their name. Buy it directly — from Namecheap, GoDaddy, or wherever — and give the developer access to build the site, not to own the domain.

**Cost: $10–$15/year.** Worth every penny of control.

### 2. Set up hosting in your own name

The hosting account should be yours. The developer should have access as a collaborator or a temporary user, but you own the account.

**Cost: $5–$30/month.** Same principle.

### 3. Get all credentials before final payment

Make it a clause in your contract: "Final payment is contingent on full admin access being handed over." Then actually verify it before you pay.

**What to receive:**
- Hosting panel login (with your own account created)
- Domain registrar login (with the domain in your name)
- WordPress admin login (as an admin user, not just a contributor)
- FTP/SSH credentials
- Any third-party service logins (email, CRM, analytics)
- Documentation of what was built and how

A good developer will hand all of this over without being asked. A bad one will drag their feet. Either way, you'll know before you're stuck.

## What to say to the developer who does respond

If they eventually respond — even after weeks of silence — assume the best. Ask:

1. Why didn't you respond? (Legitimate reasons exist: health, personal, technical)
2. Can you hand over full admin access?
3. Can you document what was built?

Don't be aggressive. The goal is to get access, not to win an argument. Even if you're furious, being calm gets you further than being hostile.

**If they're still slow or evasive** — proceed with recovery steps. Don't wait indefinitely.

## What happens if you can't get it back

The reality: some websites are lost. Not because the developer stole them, but because the records are unclear, the recovery process is slow, or the developer simply has vanished.

If that happens:

- **You start over.** It's painful. It costs time and money. But it's not the end.
- **You keep the domain** if you can recover it (best case) — the SEO history, brand recognition, and existing bookmarks continue.
- **You keep your business.** The website is a tool, not the business itself.

The clients who recover best from this are the ones who act fast, document everything, and treat it as a technical problem to solve rather than a personal betrayal to stew over.

## A final note on trust

Most freelancers are honest, reliable, and professional. The vast majority deliver what they promise and stay available for years.

But not all of them do. And the good freelancers know this — which is why they *expect* clients to want access, contracts, and ownership. A developer who resists you owning your own site is telling you something.

When you hire the next one — insist on ownership from day one. Not as a sign of distrust, but as a sign that you've learned what good professional relationships look like.
