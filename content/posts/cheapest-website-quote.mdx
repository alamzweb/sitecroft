When you're looking for someone to build your business website, getting a few quotes is usually a good idea.

You might receive one for $150, another for $500, and a third for $1,200.

If all three developers are offering to build a website, the $150 quote looks like the obvious choice. Why pay more for something that sounds like the same service?

Here's the problem: they probably aren't offering the same thing.

One quote might include a complete website with mobile optimisation, contact forms, basic SEO, security setup, and a proper handover. Another might cover only a basic design, with almost everything else charged separately.

Both developers can honestly say they're building you a website.

But what you receive at the end can be very different.

The price of a website matters. But the more important question is what you're actually getting for that price.

## The problem with comparing quotes

Most business owners compare website quotes by looking at the final number.

That's understandable. Budgets matter, especially for small businesses.

But a website project isn't a product with one standard price. The cost depends on its pages, functionality, design requirements, integrations, content, and the amount of work involved.

Consider these two hypothetical quotes:

| | Developer A | Developer B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Initial price | $200 | $650 |
| Pages | 5 | 5 |
| Mobile optimisation | Not specified | Included |
| Contact form | Extra | Included |
| Basic SEO setup | Not specified | Included |
| Revisions | 1 | 3 |
| Training and handover | Not included | Included |

Developer A looks cheaper by $450.

But the comparison changes when you start adding the things that weren't included. You might need to pay extra for the contact form, mobile adjustments, additional revisions, or help setting up the website.

Developer B's higher quote may actually cover more of what you need from the beginning.

The lesson isn't that the more expensive developer is automatically better. It's that you need to compare the scope of the work, not just the price.

## 1. Check exactly what's included

Before accepting any website quote, ask for a clear breakdown of what's included.

At a minimum, you should understand:

- How many pages will be built?
- Is the design custom or based on a template?
- Will the website work properly on mobile devices?
- Are contact forms and other required functionality included?
- Is basic on-page SEO part of the project?
- Will the developer add your content and images?
- How many rounds of revisions are allowed?
- Will you receive access to the website and its files?
- Is the website tested before launch?

These details matter because vague promises create misunderstandings.

For example, "SEO included" doesn't necessarily mean a complete SEO strategy. It might only mean setting page titles and meta descriptions. Similarly, "responsive design" should mean the actual pages are tested across different screen sizes, not simply that the developer used a responsive theme.

Get the details in writing.

A good developer should be able to explain what they're delivering without making you guess.

## 2. Understand what's not included

The things missing from a quote can be just as important as the things included.

Some costs are separate from development itself. That's normal. But you should know about them before the project starts.

Common additional expenses include:

**Domain name:** Your website needs an address, such as yourbusiness.com. Domain registration usually has a recurring renewal cost.

**Hosting:** Your website needs somewhere to run. Hosting costs depend on the provider, plan, and resources your website requires. I personally use and recommend [Hostinger](https://www.hostinger.com/?REFERRALCODE=DIGITALSYSTEMZ) as a reliable hosting provider.

**Premium themes and plugins:** Some WordPress projects require paid themes, plugins, or extensions. Check whether the developer's quote includes these licences or whether you'll purchase them separately.

**Third-party services:** Email platforms, booking systems, payment providers, and other integrations may have their own fees.

**Website content:** If you don't have written content or professional images ready, you may need to pay for copywriting, photography, or stock assets.

**Ongoing maintenance:** Updates, backups, security checks, and technical support may not be included after launch.

None of these costs automatically make a developer's quote bad. The issue is when they're presented as surprises after you've already agreed to the project.

## 3. Don't confuse a cheap template with a complete website

Templates are not inherently bad.

In fact, a well-built template can be a perfectly reasonable starting point for a small business website. There's no reason to pay for completely custom design if your requirements don't justify it.

The problem is when a developer sells a basic template installation as a fully customised website.

There's a difference between installing a theme and building a website around a business's actual needs.

A proper website still needs:

- Appropriate page layouts
- Consistent branding
- Clear navigation
- Properly organised content
- Functional forms and buttons
- Mobile-friendly layouts
- Relevant calls to action
- Performance checks

A template can provide the foundation, but it doesn't automatically solve these things.

If a developer is using a template, that's fine. You should simply know what you're paying for.

## 4. Look beyond the design

A website can look impressive in a screenshot and still be a poor website.

Design is only one part of the job.

Think about what happens when someone actually uses it.

Can they find your services quickly? Does the contact form work? Can they navigate the website on a phone? Do the pages load properly? Can search engines understand the page content?

These are the things that matter once the website is in front of real visitors.

A developer who focuses only on making the homepage look attractive may overlook the details that make the website useful.

When reviewing a developer's previous work, don't just look at screenshots. Open the actual websites.

Try the navigation, visit different pages, test the forms where possible, and check how they behave on mobile.

A portfolio should demonstrate working websites, not just attractive designs.

## 5. Ask about revisions before starting

One of the easiest ways for a small website project to become more expensive is unclear revision limits.

Imagine that you've paid for a five-page website. The developer sends you the first version, but you want to change the homepage layout, adjust the service sections, and rewrite some content.

Are those changes included?

It depends on the agreement.

Some developers include a specific number of revision rounds. Others charge separately for changes outside the original scope.

Neither approach is automatically wrong. What matters is that you understand the arrangement.

Also, distinguish between a revision and a new feature.

Changing a heading or adjusting spacing is usually a revision. Adding a customer portal, booking system, or completely new page may be additional development work.

A clear agreement protects both sides.

## 6. Check what happens after launch

This is something many business owners forget to ask.

The website launches. Everything looks good. The developer sends you the link, and the project is considered complete.

Then a few weeks later, you need to update a page, fix a form, change a phone number, or troubleshoot an issue.

Who handles it?

Some developers offer post-launch support for a defined period. Others provide maintenance plans. Some consider the project finished once the website has been handed over.

Before hiring anyone, find out:

- Will you receive full administrator access?
- Will the website and its accounts belong to you?
- Can you update content yourself?
- Is any post-launch support included?
- What happens if something breaks after launch?
- Are maintenance services available separately?

You shouldn't have to depend on the original developer for every small change.

A proper handover should leave you with access to your website and a clear understanding of how to manage it.

## 7. A higher price doesn't guarantee better work either

It's important to look at both sides.

A $200 quote isn't automatically bad, and a $2,000 quote isn't automatically good.

Some developers charge more because they have greater experience, offer a more complete service, or handle more complex projects. Others simply charge more.

Price alone doesn't prove quality.

Before making a decision, look at the developer's actual work, communication, project scope, and experience with similar websites.

Pay attention to how they handle your initial enquiry.

Do they ask about your business and requirements, or immediately send a price without understanding the project?

Do they explain what's included? Are they realistic about timelines? Can they answer technical questions clearly?

The way someone handles the project before payment can tell you a lot about how they'll handle it afterward.

## What should you compare before accepting a quote?

Here's a simple checklist you can use when comparing developers.

- [ ] The number of pages is clearly defined.
- [ ] The design approach is explained.
- [ ] Mobile responsiveness is included.
- [ ] Required forms and functionality are covered.
- [ ] Content and image responsibilities are clear.
- [ ] Basic SEO requirements are defined.
- [ ] Revisions and additional charges are explained.
- [ ] Domain, hosting, and third-party costs are identified.
- [ ] The expected delivery date is agreed upon.
- [ ] Website ownership and access are confirmed.
- [ ] Post-launch support is explained.

You don't need the most expensive option to get a good website. You need a developer whose proposal actually matches your requirements.

## When is the cheapest quote a good choice?

Sometimes, choosing the lowest quote is perfectly reasonable.

If you need a simple landing page, already have your content ready, don't require complex functionality, and are comfortable managing the website yourself, a smaller project from a capable developer may be all you need.

You shouldn't pay for services you don't need.

But if you're building a website that will represent your business, generate enquiries, sell products, or support important operations, cutting corners on essential functionality can create problems later.

The right budget is the one that covers what your business actually needs.

## The honest truth

A website quote isn't just a price. It's a description of the work someone is agreeing to do.

Two developers can charge completely different amounts because they're offering completely different services.

Don't choose someone simply because their number is lower. Don't choose someone simply because their number is higher, either.

Compare the deliverables, check their previous work, understand the additional costs, and make sure you know what happens after launch.

A good website doesn't have to be expensive. But it should be built properly, with a clear scope and no unpleasant surprises.

If you're currently comparing developers, take the time to understand each proposal before making a decision. Spending an extra few minutes asking questions now can save you a lot of frustration later.
