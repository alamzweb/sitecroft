One of the most common questions clients ask before starting a project is: *"How long will this take?"*

It's a fair question. It's also one where you'll often get either an unrealistic answer ("a couple of days!") or an evasive one ("it depends").

Here's the honest version — a realistic breakdown of how long websites actually take to build, based on what's typical for each category.

## The short answer

For a small business website:

- **Landing page:** 3–7 days
- **Standard 5–10 page site:** 1–3 weeks
- **E-commerce store:** 2–5 weeks
- **Custom-coded site:** 2–6 weeks
- **Complex web app:** 1–3 months+

Those numbers assume the client provides content on time, revisions are reasonable, and nothing unexpected happens.

Most projects take 20–40% longer than the developer's initial estimate. Not because developers are dishonest — but because clients underestimate their own contribution, and unexpected issues always come up.

## What actually takes time

Non-developers often assume that "building a website" is one big task. It's actually a dozen smaller tasks, and each one takes time.

Here's where the hours actually go:

### 1. Discovery and planning
**Time: 5–15% of the project**

Before any code is written, the developer needs to understand:

- What the site needs to do
- Who it's for
- What pages are needed
- What features are required
- What the client's brand looks like

A good developer doesn't skip this. It's the difference between building the right site and building something that needs to be redone.

### 2. Design
**Time: 15–25% of the project**

Whether it's choosing a template or creating a custom design, this is where the visual direction is set.

For a template-based site, this might be a few hours of customization. For a fully custom design, it can be a week or more.

### 3. Development
**Time: 40–60% of the project**

This is where most of the work happens. Building pages, setting up functionality, integrating tools, testing, and fixing issues.

The more complex the site, the bigger this slice becomes.

### 4. Content entry
**Time: 10–20% of the project**

Adding text, images, products, and other content to every page. This is often faster than people expect — unless the content isn't ready, in which case it becomes a bottleneck.

### 5. Testing and revisions
**Time: 10–20% of the project**

Testing on different devices, fixing bugs, and handling revisions after the client reviews the site.

Revisions can be a small part of the project, or the largest part, depending on how clear the client was upfront.

### 6. Launch
**Time: 2–5% of the project**

Final testing, DNS configuration, deployment, and handover. Often a single day for simple sites, or a week for complex ones.

## Timelines by project type

Here's a more detailed breakdown by category.

### Landing page (3–7 days)

A single-page site designed to convert visitors into leads, signups, or buyers.

**What's included:** Hero section, features or benefits, testimonials, CTA, contact form.

**Why it's fast:** Only one page, standard structure, usually little customization.

**What slows it down:** Waiting on content, complex integrations, custom animations.

### Standard business site (1–3 weeks)

A multi-page site presenting a business — home, about, services, contact, and a few more pages.

**What's included:** 5–10 pages, contact form, basic SEO, mobile-responsive design.

**Why it's medium speed:** More pages to build, more decisions to make, more content to write.

**What slows it down:** Client delays on content, lots of revisions, unexpected feature requests.

### E-commerce store (2–5 weeks)

An online store with products, cart, checkout, and payment processing.

**What's included:** Product catalog, cart, checkout, payment gateway, order management, customer accounts (sometimes).

**Why it takes longer:** Every product needs to be added. Every edge case (shipping, taxes, discounts) needs to be handled. Payment integration takes time to test.

**What slows it down:** A large product catalog, complex shipping rules, custom checkout flows.

### Custom-coded site (2–6 weeks)

A website built from scratch using React, Next.js, or similar frameworks.

**What's included:** Custom design, hand-coded pages, custom interactions, deployment to Vercel or similar.

**Why it takes longer:** No pre-built components to reuse. Every page, feature, and animation is built from scratch.

**What slows it down:** Custom features, complex interactions, integrations with external services.

### Web app or platform (1–3 months+)

A full application — dashboards, user accounts, real-time features, databases, and complex logic.

**What's included:** Everything you'd expect from a website plus real functionality.

**Why it takes the longest:** Every feature is a mini-project. Real testing is required. Edge cases multiply.

**What slows it down:** Every additional feature, every integration, every unexpected requirement.

## What actually makes projects take longer

Here's the honest list of what causes delays, in order of frequency:

### 1. Content isn't ready
By far the biggest cause. Developers can build fast, but they can't write your copy, take your photos, or choose your brand voice.

If you want a project done in 2 weeks, have your content ready before the developer starts.

### 2. Scope creep
Small additions feel harmless in the moment. "Can you also add a blog?" "Can we also include a booking system?"

Each one adds days. Multiple small additions can turn a 2-week project into a 6-week one.

### 3. Slow feedback
Every revision round requires the client to review, give feedback, wait for changes, and review again. If the client takes a week to give feedback, the project takes a week longer.

### 4. Underestimated complexity
Some features look simple but aren't. A "simple" contact form with conditional logic and email routing can take a full day. A "small" redesign can cascade into major changes.

### 5. Third-party delays
Hosting setup, payment gateway approval, DNS propagation, SSL certificates — external services sometimes take days to sort out.

## How to make your project faster

If you want your website done quickly, here's what actually helps:

**1. Have your content ready.**
All of it. Text, images, products, everything. Before the developer starts.

**2. Be decisive.**
Every "let me think about it" adds time. Have a clear vision upfront.

**3. Give feedback quickly.**
When the developer sends something for review, review it that week — not next month.

**4. Limit revisions.**
Two rounds of revisions is normal. Five is a sign of unclear brief. Eight means the project is in trouble.

**5. Don't add scope mid-project.**
Every addition delays the original goal. Save it for phase two.

**6. Trust the process.**
Developers who've done this before know what they're doing. Fighting them on structure, timeline, or approach usually makes things slower, not faster.

## Realistic vs unrealistic expectations

A few examples of common client expectations vs. reality:

| Client says | Reality |
|---|---|
| "It's just a small site — a few days?" | Only if content is ready and there are no changes |
| "Can we also add..." (mid-project) | Adds 2–7 days depending on scope |
| "The competitor site took a week" | Theirs was a template install, not a build |
| "We need it live by Friday" | Possible for a landing page, unrealistic for a store |
| "We're not sure about the design yet" | Then the project hasn't started yet |

## The realistic timeline for most small businesses

For a typical small business website — WordPress, 5–10 pages, standard contact form, basic SEO — here's the honest timeline:

- **Week 1:** Discovery, content gathering, initial setup
- **Week 2:** Design, build, first draft
- **Week 3:** Revisions, testing, launch

Three weeks is realistic if content is ready from the start.

If the client provides content slowly, or asks for many changes, it can stretch to 5–6 weeks.

## What to do with this information

**If you're a client:**
Use these timelines as a rough guide. Ask your developer for their specific estimate — but be suspicious of anyone promising significantly faster than this. Either they're cutting corners, or they don't know what they're doing.

**If you're a developer:**
Use these timelines to give clients realistic expectations upfront. Always buffer the estimate — you'll always use the buffer. And communicate early if you're going to be late.

## Bottom line

Websites take time. The specific timeline depends on complexity, but here's the honest range for a small business:

- **Fastest realistic:** 3 days (landing page, content ready)
- **Typical:** 2–3 weeks (standard site)
- **Slower:** 5–8 weeks (complex site, slow content)

Anyone promising significantly faster is probably overselling.

Anyone telling you it "depends on everything" isn't giving you a real answer.

Use the ranges in this post to set your own expectations, and ask your developer to give you their specific timeline with confidence.
