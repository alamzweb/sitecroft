You've decided you need a website. Now comes the harder question: who builds it?

The two obvious answers are a **freelancer** or an **agency**. Both exist for good reasons, and both are wrong for the wrong client.

Most articles on this topic pretend there's a clear winner. There isn't. What there is: two very different ways of working, and a decision that depends almost entirely on what you actually need.

Here's an honest breakdown.

## The short answer

- **Freelancers** are usually better for smaller projects, tighter budgets, and clients who want direct communication with the person doing the work.
- **Agencies** are usually better for larger projects, multi-disciplinary work, or businesses that need the safety net of a team.
- **The best choice is almost always determined by project size and how much you value direct communication.**

## The real differences

Most comparisons focus on price. That's part of it — but not the most important part. Here are the actual things that change depending on who you hire.

### Cost

Freelancers are almost always cheaper. That's because you're paying one person, with low overhead, who might work from anywhere.

A typical freelance web developer might charge **$150–$1,500** for a small business website.
An agency for the same project might charge **$1,500–$5,000+**.

The gap comes from agency overhead — account managers, office space, project coordinators, senior staff who review work. You're paying for structure and reliability. Sometimes that's worth it. Sometimes it isn't.

### Speed

**Freelancers can be faster for small projects.** There's no internal back-and-forth, no coordinating between roles, no waiting for a designer to finish before the developer can start.

**Agencies are often faster for large projects.** Their structure means multiple people work in parallel. A designer, developer, and content writer can each do their part simultaneously.

For a single-page site: a freelancer might finish in 3 days; an agency might take 2 weeks.
For a full rebrand with 30 pages and e-commerce: a freelancer might take 2 months; an agency might take 3 weeks.

The pattern: **freelancers win on small, agencies win on large.**

### Communication

**This is the biggest practical difference.**

With a freelancer, you talk to the person doing the work. Questions get answered quickly. Feedback goes directly to the person who'll act on it. There's no filter.

With an agency, you usually talk to an account manager or project lead. Feedback goes through layers. Sometimes that's fine. Sometimes it's frustrating — especially when a small change takes a week to get through the pipeline.

**If direct communication matters to you, a freelancer wins.**
**If you'd rather not manage the details, an agency wins.**

### Reliability and risk

**Agencies have redundancy.** If your contact person is sick, someone else can step in. If the designer quits, the project continues. There's an infrastructure protecting your project.

**Freelancers don't have this.** If your freelancer has a medical emergency, disappears, or goes on vacation at the wrong time — your project stalls.

This is the honest downside of freelancing. The vast majority of freelancers are reliable and professional. But the risk profile is different. With an agency, you're paying for a system. With a freelancer, you're trusting one person.

**How to mitigate this:** look at their portfolio, talk to past clients, and don't skip the contract. Freelancers who have a clear contract and a documented process are much less risky than freelancers who don't.

### Accountability

**Agencies have a formal chain of command.** If something goes wrong, there's a manager you can escalate to. There are processes. There's a business behind the work.

**Freelancers can be harder to hold accountable** — not because they aren't professional, but because there's no structure above them. If you're unhappy, your options are limited to what your contract says.

**The flip side:** freelancers have much more to lose from a bad review. Every client is a referral source. Most freelancers are extremely protective of their reputation, precisely because they don't have an agency brand to fall back on.

### Expertise

**Agencies can offer specialists** — a dedicated SEO person, a copywriter, a brand designer, a motion designer. Each does what they're best at.

**Freelancers are usually generalists.** A solo web developer often handles design, development, and basic SEO themselves. They may not be best-in-class at every element. But they'll be competent across all of them.

For a project that needs strong specialists (a brand identity + custom e-commerce + content strategy), an agency is the obvious answer.

For a project that needs one strong person to build a good website, a freelancer is often a better match.

## When a freelancer is the right choice

**Choose a freelancer if:**

- Your project is small to medium (5–15 pages, standard functionality)
- Your budget is tight — you'd rather spend less and accept a bit more risk
- You want direct communication with the person doing the work
- You don't need specialists — one capable developer/designer is enough
- You have clear requirements and can make decisions quickly
- You're comfortable managing a project yourself (or don't mind learning)

**Freelancers are almost always the right choice for:**
- Portfolio sites
- Small business brochure sites
- Single landing pages
- WordPress sites under 15 pages
- Any project where "good and fast" beats "perfect but delayed"

## When an agency is the right choice

**Choose an agency if:**

- Your project is large or complex (multi-language, e-commerce, custom integrations)
- You need specialists — brand strategy, UX research, copywriting, motion, etc.
- You want minimal involvement — you'd rather not manage the project yourself
- You need guaranteed timelines with contractual backing
- The project's failure would be costly (rebrand, major platform migration)
- You have a budget for it — you're paying more, but getting more structure

**Agencies are almost always the right choice for:**
- Full rebrands
- Complex e-commerce builds
- Enterprise websites
- Projects that need designers + developers + strategists working together
- Anything that must launch on a strict deadline

## The middle path — small studios

There's a third option that gets ignored: **a small studio of 2–4 people.**

Small studios often combine the best of both:
- Direct communication (like a freelancer)
- A bit of redundancy (like an agency)
- Specialists when needed (a designer and developer, usually)
- Mid-range pricing (between freelancer and agency rates)

For a project that's "bigger than one person but not an enterprise" — a 15-page site with a small e-commerce section, or a brand refresh plus a new website — a small studio can be the ideal fit.

## How to actually decide

Stop asking "which is better" and ask these five questions:

**1. How big is the project?**
Under 15 pages → freelancer or small studio.
15+ pages or complex functionality → small studio or agency.
Multi-phase, multi-discipline → agency.

**2. What's the budget?**
Under $1,000 → freelancer.
$1,000–$5,000 → freelancer or small studio.
$5,000+ → small studio or agency.

**3. How important is direct communication to you?**
Very → freelancer.
Moderately → small studio.
Not really, just get it done → agency.

**4. Do you need specialists you can't hire individually?**
No → freelancer.
Maybe one or two → small studio.
Yes, several → agency.

**5. What's the cost of the project failing?**
Low (a personal site) → freelancer.
Medium (a small business site) → freelancer or small studio.
High (a rebrand, a major platform) → agency.

## What I'd actually recommend

**For most small business websites, a freelancer or small studio is the right choice.** The cost savings are real, the communication is better, and the work quality — from a good freelancer — matches what you'd get from an agency at 3–5x the price.

**But don't pick a freelancer just to save money.** Pick one because you actually want the kind of working relationship they offer — direct, personal, and fast.

**If your project is genuinely large or complex, don't try to hire a freelancer just to save money.** You'll either get a freelancer who's out of their depth, or you'll end up paying for the agency you should have hired — after the project goes sideways.

The right choice isn't about "freelancer or agency." It's about matching the shape of your project to the shape of the provider.

## One last thing

Regardless of who you hire:

- **Get a written contract.** Always.
- **Talk to past clients.** Always.
- **Be clear about scope.** Vague projects go badly, always.
- **Pay a deposit.** Anyone good will ask for one.
- **Trust your gut.** If a call feels off, it is.

The best provider for your project is the one you can actually communicate with. Everything else is secondary.
